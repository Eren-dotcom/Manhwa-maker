# 04 · Panel direction (연출)

> The panel director decides what the reader sees, from where, at what size, and in what order. In Korean studios this is a named job — *yeonchul* — and it is the difference between an illustrated script and a comic.

## Shot grammar

| Shot | What it is for | Failure mode |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Establishing** | Place, scale, time of day. Opens a sequence or a new location. | Used for every scene; becomes wallpaper. |
| **Wide** | Full bodies in space; blocking and geography. | Figures too small to read emotion. |
| **Full** | One body, full height; action readability. | Cropped feet break the silhouette. |
| **Medium** | Waist up; the default for conversation and business. | The whole episode becomes this. |
| **Medium close** | Chest up; emotional exchange with hands visible. | Hands disappear and the acting disappears with them. |
| **Close-up** | Face; the moment a line lands. | Overused, so nothing feels close. |
| **Extreme close** | One detail at maximum pressure: eyes, lips, a trembling hand. | Twice per episode, maximum. |
| **Insert** | An object that carries information. | Used decoratively, not as a clue. |
| **Over-shoulder** | Power imbalance between two people. | Shoulder blocks the subject's face. |
| **Silhouette** | Conceal identity, promise threat, iconography. | No information at all; the reader stops caring. |
| **Panel-less / bleed** | Breath or catastrophe. | No frame on every panel — the shock dies. |

**The rhythm rule:** never use the same shot twice in a row unless the repetition *is* the joke or the dread. A sequence of medium→medium→medium is a radio play.

## Angle is psychology

- **Eye level:** equal footing. The default for honest scenes.
- **Low angle:** power, threat, heroism — the camera is below the character's eyeline, so they loom.
- **High angle:** vulnerability, judgement, being watched.
- **Bird's eye:** isolation, strategy, scale, or evidence laid out.
- **Worm's eye:** monumentality; use for architecture and for a character becoming a myth.
- **Dutch tilt:** instability and wrongness. Never for a whole scene.
- **Top-down:** ritual, tableau, a body on the floor.

Angle changes within a conversation are the cheapest way to show who is winning. Start at eye level; drop to a low angle when one character takes control; raise to a high angle when they are exposed.

## Composition

| Composition | Effect | Where it belongs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Centre | Confrontation, icon frames | Standoffs, declarations |
| Rule of thirds | Safe default; room for balloons | Conversation, business |
| Diagonal | Motion and urgency in a still image | Chases, impacts, panic |
| Symmetry | Order, ritual, authority, dread | Institutions, ceremonies, the uncanny |
| Negative space | Loneliness and breathing room | Grief, silence, the scroll after a reveal |
| Frame within frame | Surveillance, entrapment, watched lives | Doorways, windows, mirrors |
| Claustrophobic crop | Pressure | Panic attacks, interrogation |
| Leading lines | Pulls the eye down the scroll | Corridors, stairs, rivers, roads |

In vertical scroll, **leading lines always point down** unless you are deliberately fighting the format. A diagonal from upper-left to lower-right is the most natural motion path the eye will follow.

## The camera must obey physics (the 180° rule)

Two characters facing each other share an imaginary line. Keep the camera on one side of it, or the characters will appear to swap sides between panels and the reader will feel lost without knowing why.

Also track:

- **Eyelines.** If A looks down-right at B in panel 4, B in panel 5 must be lower and to the left, looking up-left.
- **Screen direction.** If the hero runs right, they keep running right until a cut tells us otherwise.
- **Light direction.** One established key light per scene. Two contradicting shadows read as an error, not a mood.

## Vertical continuity inside one scroll

Because the reader scrolls rather than turns, the bottom of panel N is physically adjacent to the top of panel N+1. Use that:

- **Match the exit and the entry.** A character exits frame bottom-right; the next panel opens bottom-right on what they walked into.
- **Cascade the eye.** Place the subject of N+1 slightly lower than the subject of N, so the eye is pulled down rather than asked to jump.
- **Cut on action.** Panel N begins the swing; panel N+1 is the impact. The reader fills the missing frame and feels the speed.
- **Use a tall panel to stop time.** A 2× panel is a held breath. Follow it with a 0.6× snap.

## Staging action

1. **Silhouette first.** The pose must read as a black shape at 120px.
2. **One idea per panel.** A punch is a panel; the reaction is the next panel. Never both.
3. **Impact frames are whites and blacks.** Prioritise contrast over detail; add speed lines that follow the motion arc, not the frame edge.
4. **Show the weight.** Feet, grip, cloth, debris. Action without contact reads as floaty.
5. **Choreograph in space first.** Sketch the floor plan, arrows, and distances before choosing any shot. Most bad fights are a staging problem, not a drawing problem.
6. **Let the reader breathe.** After three panels of violence, give one panel of a shoe on gravel.

## Silence and negative space

The single most underused tool in AI-assisted comics is the panel with nothing in it. A fogged river with one lantern is a stronger character beat than a face saying "I'm afraid".

Rules:

- A silent panel must still change something — information, mood, or geometry.
- Three silent panels in a row need a deliberate progression (wide → closer → detail), or they read as un-lettered.
- Leave the upper third clear when a caption is coming; leave the lower third clear when the next panel needs to be discovered.

## The thumbnail test

Every panel should be legible as a 120px-wide thumbnail. Check:

- Can you count the characters?
- Can you tell where they are looking?
- Is the change from the previous panel visible?

If not: increase contrast, simplify the background, enlarge the subject, and cut one element.

## A practical direction pass

1. Read the beat sheet out loud. Mark the emotional peak of each unit.
2. Thumbnail the whole episode at 200px wide, 8 panels per unit. Do not draw detail.
3. Check the rhythm bars: at least four different heights, at least two full-bleeds, one 2× splash.
4. Choose one **signature image** per episode — the panel that will be the thumbnail, the poster and the tweet.
5. Only then write the action lines, in present tense, one instant per panel.
6. Read the strip on a phone before you spend a single render.
