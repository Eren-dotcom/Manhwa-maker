# 03 · Writing craft for weekly vertical

> A webtoon is not a novel with pictures. It is a sequence of images that a reader consumes at thumb speed, and the writing exists to make those images inevitable.

## The pitch, in the order editors read it

1. **Title** — concrete, imageable, and searchable. *The Lantern Bureau* beats *Eternal Nightfall*.
2. **One-line logline** — `[protagonist] must [goal] or [stakes], but [obstacle]`. Under 35 words. If it takes two sentences, the concept is not finished.
3. **Hook** — one image or one line the reader meets in the first three panels. *"Every lantern in the city has gone dark. Except the one in his hand."*
4. **Genre + engine** — the promise of repeatable pleasure: hunter gates, contract romance, regression, sports mastery.
5. **Comparables** — two existing hits the platform can use to position you.
6. **Episode 1 outline** — 8 units, not 8 chapters.

A logline fails when it contains abstractions ("must find the strength within") or when the obstacle is not physical. Ask: *what would the trailer show?*

## Want, need, flaw — the engine

| Element | What it is | Test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Want** | What the character pursues and states out loud | Could you film the pursuit? |
| **Need** | What the story proves they require | Does the want conflict with it? |
| **Flaw** | The reason they cannot have both | Does it cost them something on the page in episode 1? |
| **Arc** | The change the flaw forces | Can you name the moment of choice? |

If want and need are compatible, you have a plot, not a story. If the flaw costs nothing visible, you have a description, not a character.

**Korean-register nuance:** hierarchy, age and obligation are part of the flaw. A character who cannot speak casually to someone younger is revealing something about status, family and fear. Use register as characterisation, not decoration.

## Scenes: enter late, leave early

A scene in a webtoon is 4–8 panels, not a chapter. Construct it like this:

1. **Enter 30 seconds after the argument starts.** Open on the line or the image that is already too hot.
2. **One desire per scene.** Someone wants something in this room and someone blocks it.
3. **Escalate three times.** Each beat raises the cost or reveals a new obstacle.
4. **Leave before the resolution.** Cut on the unanswered question — the reader scrolls to the next scene for relief.

If a scene can be summarised as "they talk about what they're going to do", cut it and open at the doing.

## Dialogue rules that survive the format

- **≤35 words per panel, ≤12 words per balloon.** The phone screen is the budget.
- **One idea per balloon.** If it needs a comma and a conjunction, it is two balloons.
- **Subtext over statement.** A character who says exactly what they feel is a first draft. Give them a lie that is almost true.
- **Interruptions are free pacing.** An em dash at a panel break buys you a scroll.
- **Silence is a line.** A panel with no text is a beat; the art carries it. Do not letter a panel whose image already says the thing.
- **Names as punctuation.** A character's full name in a balloon is a slap. Save it.
- **Avoid exposition by making information a weapon.** Nobody explains the rules; someone uses the rules to win an argument.

### Korean dialogue conventions to keep in translation

- **Register shift** (존댓말 → 반말) is a relationship milestone — mark it, do not smooth it out.
- **Indirect refusal** ("그건 좀…", "I'll think about it") is a real refusal. Do not translate it as hesitation to be overwritten by the plot.
- **Address forms carry information.** Name + 씨 (-ssi), title + 님 (-nim), 선배 (sunbae), 형/오빠 (hyung/oppa). Keep what carries relationship; drop what does not.
- **Honorific verbs** have no English equivalent. Translate the *effect*: shorter sentences for rudeness, formal constructions for distance.

## The weekly structure (45 panels)

| Panels | Function | Writing job |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1–4 | Hook | One image, one rule, one person breaking it |
| 5–12 | Setup | Daily procedure, competence, small human detail |
| 13–20 | Cast | Pressure character arrives; polite war |
| 21–28 | Turn | The rules fail; a visual anomaly |
| 29–34 | Escalate | Cost at scale; the world widens |
| 35–40 | Reveal | Personal stake; a name is spoken |
| 41–43 | Breather | Silence; one prop; one thought |
| 44–45 | Stinger | Contact; cut; no explanation |

At 30 panels (WEBTOON global), compress setup and escalate; never compress the breather or the stinger.

## Genre engines, and how to break them usefully

- **Hunter/System:** the numbers are a promise of fairness. Break it by making the system *unfair on purpose* — a stat that cannot be spent, a rank that lies.
- **Regression:** the protagonist knows the future, so the tension must come from *people*, not events. What did they get wrong about the people they saved?
- **Contract romance:** the fake relationship is a machine for forced proximity. The obstacle must be internal (shame, class, grief) or the reader stops believing it.
- **Bullying revenge:** violence without moral cost is pornography. Give every act a receipt.
- **Isekai:** the joke is the mismatch between the protagonist's competence and the world's expectations. The moment the mismatch disappears, the series ends — so pace the reveal of what they actually are.

## Foreshadowing with objects, not narration

- Plant 3 props per episode that can pay off later. A chipped badge, a name on wet paper, a route slip.
- A prop is foreshadowing only if it is **looked at** on panel — give it a close-up or an insert.
- Payoff must be *physical*. "He remembered his promise" is not a payoff; the lantern is the payoff.

## Common first-episode failures

| Failure | Symptom | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lore dump | Three captions explaining the world before anyone moves | One rule, shown. The rest arrives when it costs someone something. |
| Waking up in bed | Panel 1 is a ceiling | Open on the irreversible thing. |
| Weak hook | Panel 3 is a conversation about logistics | Replace with an image the reader cannot unsee. |
| No cliffhanger | Episode ends with a plan | End with the plan already failing. |
| Protagonist passive | Things happen *to* them for 40 panels | Give them one decision in unit 3 that makes things worse. |
| All dialogue, no silence | Every panel has three balloons | Delete the middle balloon in every panel and re-read. |

## Chapter-to-chapter continuity

Maintain a running **open questions** list. Every episode should close one question and open two. If the list is shrinking, the series is ending — which is fine, but it should be a decision, not an accident.
