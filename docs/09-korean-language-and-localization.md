# 09 · Korean language & localisation

> You do not need to write in Korean to make a convincing manhwa. You do need to know what Korean is doing to a scene, or the English will read like a translated instruction manual.

## Names

- **Family name first.** Han Yuwol = family name Han (한), given name Yuwol (유월).
- Common family names: Kim, Lee, Park, Choi, Jung/Jeong, Kang, Cho/Jo, Yoon, Jang, Lim, Han, Oh, Seo, Shin, Kwon, Hwang, Ahn, Song.
- Given names are usually two syllables, often nature- or virtue-based: Yuwol (June), Soryeong, Jiwoo, Minjun, Haneul (sky), Bomi (spring).
- Romanisation: Revised Romanisation is standard (Kim, Lee, Park — though the *sounds* are Gim, I, Bak). Be consistent; do not mix systems in one series.
- **How a character is addressed is a plot fact.** Full name + 씨 is formal-distance; given name alone is intimate or rude depending on age; a title (선배, 팀장님) states hierarchy.

## Register: the engine of Korean dialogue

Korean has two main speech levels in daily use:

| Level | Form | Used between |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **존댓말 (jondaemal)** | Polite/formal endings (-요, -습니다) | Strangers, superiors, professional contexts, anyone you respect or fear |
| **반말 (banmal)** | Casual endings | Close friends of the same age, family, children, or someone you are deliberately disrespecting |

**A register switch is an event.** When two characters finally drop to banmal, the reader feels the relationship change. When one refuses to switch, that is a wall. When someone uses banmal at a stranger, it is an insult or a threat.

Write the switch into your beat sheet. Do not let a localiser smooth it out.

## Honorifics worth keeping in translation

| Term | Meaning | Keep? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **-씨 (-ssi)** | Polite suffix after a name | Keep — it signals distance |
| **-님 (-nim)** | Respectful suffix (also after titles) | Keep |
| **선배 / 후배** | Senior / junior in school or work | Keep, it is structural |
| **형 / 오빠** | Older brother (male speaker / female speaker) | Keep if the intimacy matters; gloss in a note |
| **누나 / 언니** | Older sister (male / female speaker) | Keep, same rule |
| **아저씨 / 아주머니** | Middle-aged man/woman, sometimes rude | Translate the effect, not the word |
| **-아 / -야** | Vocative after a name (casual) | Drop in English; the casual register carries it |

## What does *not* translate

- **Honorific verb endings.** There is no English equivalent. Translate the effect: shorter, plainer sentences for rudeness; longer, hedged sentences for formality.
- **Indirect refusal.** "그건 좀…" or "생각해 볼게요" is a refusal. Do not translate it as an opening.
- **Age-based hierarchy inside friendships.** The oldest speaks last in an argument; the youngest apologises first. Show it in staging, not in a footnote.
- **Kinship terms used socially.** A stranger calling you 언니 is intimacy or salesmanship, not family.

## Cultural texture that reads as authentic

- **Food:** ramen at a convenience store, a lunch box left on a desk, a shared tangerine, soju with the label peeled. Meals are how care is expressed.
- **Spaces:** shoe racks at the door, ondol floors (people sit on the floor), apartment corridors with one flickering tube light, PC bang glow, subway line colours.
- **Work:** 회식 (company dinners), team leads (팀장님), overtime, the fluorescent-lit office at 11pm.
- **School:** class numbers (2반), the seating chart as social map, hagwon at night, uniform ties loosened.
- **Weather as season:** 장마 (monsoon) in summer, first snow as a romantic marker, yellow dust in spring.

## Localisation workflow

1. Write the **source episode** with Korean-specific terms marked in the bible glossary.
2. Produce a **translation note** per episode: register choices, honorifics kept, puns, cultural references.
3. Localise dialogue, not word count — but check text expansion (Korean → English grows 10–20%). Leave 15% slack in balloons.
4. Re-letter for the target language; do not overlay.
5. Check fonts for Hangul + Latin coverage if names are mixed (Noto Sans KR is a safe default).
6. For RTL languages, mirror *panels*, not the whole strip, and redraw any SFX or diegetic text.

## Writing English that sounds Korean, not translated

- Prefer shorter sentences. Korean dialogue is often clipped.
- Use silence and one-word answers; they carry more weight than in English prose.
- Let characters state facts instead of feelings ("The river keeps its own time").
- Avoid "as you know" exposition; hierarchy is expressed through action, not summary.
- Keep a small number of Korean words untranslated — the ones that carry relationship — and define them once, in the glossary.

## The line to draw

Specificity is not exoticism. A lantern, a ledger stamp, or a shoe rack is authentic because it is *functional* in the scene. Ornament piled on for flavour — hanbok in a modern office, Hangul pasted onto signs at random — reads as costume, and readers from Korea will notice immediately.
